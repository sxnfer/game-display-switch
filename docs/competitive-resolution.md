# Competitive resolution

[← Back to the README](../README.md)


Some people play shooters on a smaller area than their monitor, say 25" of a 27" panel, with a low stretched resolution inside it. Display Switch can do that per game: while the game runs, the desktop switches to a resolution of your choice, shown **1:1 in the middle of the screen with black borders**, so the monitor behaves like a smaller one. When the game closes, the screen goes back to its normal resolution. It is independent of HDR: a game can have HDR, a resolution, both or neither, and a game you set to "never HDR" can still have a resolution.

In the flyout's **Games** tab, click a game to open its page and turn on **Competitive area**. The list that opens shows **Off** (the whole screen), then every resolution your display offers at its current refresh rate, the ones with the screen's own aspect ratio first, each with how much of the screen it covers (for example `92.5 % · 24.6″`). Windows reports refresh rates in whole hertz, and the same rate can show as 239 Hz on one size and 240 Hz on another, so the list takes a size that is 1 Hz off the rate as being at that rate (the switch does the same). Sizes the display has only at other rates are greyed out at the end of the list and say at which (`only at 60 Hz`): they can't be picked until they exist at the rate the game is switched at. Pick one and it is saved, and applied right away if the game is running. Back on the page, the card shows your screen to scale with the area drawn 1:1 in the middle, its size and diagonal (`2368 × 1332 · 24.5″`), and the size with **More…** to pick another and **Create…** to make one the display doesn't have yet; the switch turns it off again. If the display doesn't offer that size at the rate it switches at, a yellow bar on the page says what to create ([Creating the resolution](#creating-the-resolution)); after a switch that didn't go as the page says (a missing resolution, a stretch that wasn't possible, another refresh rate), the bar shows the latest notification about it until the game is next switched. The flyout's **Live** tab shows the active resolution as the **Competitive area** layer (and a stretch as **Stretch**), and the tray icon's tooltip shows it next to the HDR state, for example `2368 × 1332 – Counter-Strike 2`.

## Creating the resolution

The resolution has to exist before Display Switch can switch to it: Windows only switches to sizes the monitor is known to have. Display Switch can add one for you.

**Create a size.** On the game's page, click **Create…** on the Competitive area card (or **Create a size…** under the list of sizes). Then:

1. **Screen size**: drag the slider to the diagonal you want, in 0.1″ steps from 15″ up to your panel's own size (the arrow keys move a tenth, Page Up and Page Down a whole inch). Display Switch reads the panel's size from the monitor itself.
2. **Shape**: your screen's own (*Native*), 16:9, 16:10, 4:3 or 5:4.
3. **Refresh rate**: your desktop's current rate, or another one the monitor has at its full resolution. A resolution the GPU scales can only use a rate the monitor has at native. The new resolution gets the panel's exact rate (for example 239.970 Hz, not a round 240): its timing is set so that its rate is never below the native mode's, and at most a few thousandths of a hertz above it, so Windows lists both at the same whole rate.

Above the controls, the picture shows the size to scale on your screen, and the line under it what it comes to, for example `2368 × 1332 · 24.51″ · 92.5 %`: the largest resolution of that shape that still reads as the diagonal you chose, with a width that is a multiple of 8. If the display already has that size, the button says **Use it** and nothing has to be created. Otherwise **Create…** shows what is about to happen, and **Create and restart driver** does it. A few seconds later the game has its new size.

**It needs CRU, once.** Display Switch adds the resolution the way [Custom Resolution Utility (CRU)](https://www.monitortests.com/forum/Thread-Custom-Resolution-Utility-CRU) by ToastyX does, and uses CRU's own `restart64.exe` to restart the graphics driver. CRU is free; Display Switch neither includes nor downloads it. Download it from [its page](https://www.monitortests.com/forum/Thread-Custom-Resolution-Utility-CRU), unzip it anywhere, and choose that folder when the creator asks (**Open download page** and **Choose folder…**), or in **Settings › Resolutions › CRU folder**. The folder has to contain `CRU.exe` and `restart64.exe` (`restart.exe` on 32-bit Windows). You never have to open CRU itself. This also gets round NVIDIA Control Panel's limits on custom resolutions.

**What it does to your PC, exactly.** A monitor tells Windows which resolutions it has in a small block of data, its EDID. Windows lets that be overridden in the registry, under `HKLM\SYSTEM\CurrentControlSet\Enum\DISPLAY\<monitor>\<instance>\Device Parameters\EDID_OVERRIDE`. That is where CRU saves its changes, and where Display Switch adds the one resolution. The monitor itself is never written to. Then the graphics driver restarts so it reads the override:

- **Your screens go black for a few seconds** while the driver restarts. Windows and your programs keep running; some windows may move. Display Switch won't start this while a game is running.
- **If the picture doesn't come back within 15 seconds, press F8.** That is CRU's recovery mode, which temporarily unloads the overrides without deleting them. Then use **Remove** (below).
- **Windows asks for administrator permission**, because the override is in a protected part of the registry. Display Switch itself keeps running without those rights: only this one step is done by a second, short-lived copy of it. Say no at the prompt and nothing is changed.
- **Monitors that use DSC** (Display Stream Compression: many 4K 144 Hz+ and 1440p 360 Hz+ monitors) can make the NVIDIA driver ignore EDID overrides. Then nothing changes: Display Switch checks afterwards that Windows really lists the new resolution, and tells you if it doesn't.

**What keeps it safe.**

- **A backup first.** Before it writes to a monitor's override for the first time, Display Switch saves what was there (whether an override existed, and every byte of it) to `%AppData%\HdrSwitch\edid-backups\`. Nothing is written if the backup can't be saved and read back.
- **Your own CRU changes stay.** If the monitor already has an override, the new resolution is added to it. An override Display Switch can't read is never written over: it tells you, and you sort it out in CRU.
- **Nothing changes behind your back.** If the monitor's EDID or override changed between the confirmation and the write (you used CRU meanwhile, say), nothing is written and you are asked to open the creator again.
- **The write is checked.** What was written is read back; if writing fails half way, what was there before is put back at once.
- **Everything is in the log.** Each step is a line in **Settings › Switch log** (`edid-apply`, `edid-backup`, `edid-write`, `edid-restart`, …), with what the EDID gained.

**Undo.** **Settings › Resolutions** lists each monitor Display Switch added resolutions to. **Remove** puts that monitor's override back exactly as the backup has it (your own override from before, or no override at all), restarts the driver, and deletes the backup. It removes everything Display Switch added to that monitor, and any change made to the override since (in CRU, for example). A game whose size is gone then says so on its page, and switches again once you pick or create another. You can also remove an override in CRU itself (**Delete**, then `restart64.exe`), or with CRU's `reset-all.exe`.

**Or create it yourself in NVIDIA Control Panel.** If you'd rather not use CRU, and NVIDIA Control Panel accepts the resolution:

1. **Display › Change resolution › Customize… › Create Custom Resolution**. Enter the size you want, at your monitor's refresh rate (not 60 Hz), test and save it. For 25" on a 27" 2560×1440 panel that is **2368 × 1332** (exactly 16:9, 92.5 % of the diagonal).

## Scaling

In NVIDIA Control Panel, **Display › Adjust desktop size and position**: keep the scaling you like for everything else, for example **Full screen**, so other games can stretch a low resolution over the whole screen. Perform scaling on **GPU**, and leave **"Override the scaling mode set by games and programs" unticked**.

While a game with a competitive resolution runs, stretched or not, Display Switch switches NVIDIA's scaling to **No scaling** itself and puts your setting back when the game closes. That is what keeps the picture centred when the game uses *exclusive* fullscreen, which sets its own display mode. The driver saves this setting like Control Panel does, so if the PC loses power mid-game, Display Switch puts it back the next time it starts. It only puts back a No scaling it set: if you change the setting yourself while the game runs, it leaves yours alone. If the driver can't be asked at that moment, that doesn't count as a change: your setting is kept and put back at the next chance (the next display change, the next game closing, the next start). After setting No scaling it reads the setting back, and if the driver didn't keep it, or it couldn't be set because the desktop wasn't at its normal resolution when the game started, a notification says so (*NVIDIA's scaling couldn't be set to No scaling…*): a game in exclusive fullscreen may then fill the whole screen. On AMD or Intel graphics this step is skipped.

## While you play

In the game, use the **same resolution as the competitive one** in exclusive fullscreen, or borderless (fullscreen windowed) mode, which always draws at the desktop's size and so fills the area. For a lower resolution stretched over the area, see the next part.

The switch happens the moment you press Play in Steam, or when the game's process appears, so the game sees the smaller desktop from the start. For a game not started through Steam, Display Switch looks for its process four times a second while no game runs. If the switch still came more than two seconds after the game started (or you set a resolution while the game is already running), a notification says so: restart the game if it doesn't fit. Three seconds after the last game with a resolution closes, the display goes back to the mode saved in Windows display settings, scaling included. The change is never saved: a reboot or sign-out always comes back at your normal resolution, and if Display Switch is killed mid-game, Windows drops the temporary mode with it (the next start switches again if the game is still running).

With more than one screen, the resolution goes on the screen the game was on last time, or the main one if it hasn't been seen yet or that screen isn't connected. If the game's window then shows on another screen, the resolution follows it about two seconds later: the screen it left goes back to its normal resolution and NVIDIA scaling, and the new one is switched (at most three times per start of the game, in case something keeps moving the window). If the new screen doesn't offer that resolution, the usual notification says what is missing. A game's page, its lists of sizes and **Create…** are about that same screen: the one the game was on last time, else the main one.

**While the game runs, the resolution is kept.** Windows and the graphics driver put the desktop back at its saved resolution for reasons of their own: HDR switching on or off, the PC or the monitor waking up, a monitor plugged in again, a permission prompt on the secure desktop, Alt+Tab out of an exclusive-fullscreen game. Display Switch notices (a second after the display stops changing, two seconds after waking, on unlocking, and in its once-a-second look at the running games) and sets the competitive resolution again, stretch and NVIDIA's No scaling included. What it does depends on what it finds:

- **The desktop is back at the saved resolution**, or the size is right but no longer centred or stretched: Windows reset it, so it is set again. At most three times a minute per game. If it is reset more often than that, something else wants that resolution (usually the game itself, in exclusive fullscreen at your normal resolution), so Display Switch stops, says so once (*Windows keeps resetting the resolution…*) and tries again only when something new happens: another display change, waking up, unlocking.
- **The desktop is at a third resolution**, neither the competitive nor the saved one: the game set its own in exclusive fullscreen. Display Switch doesn't fight a game over the display. It leaves the game's mode alone and says so once; with No scaling the game's picture is still centred. For the whole area, set the game to the competitive resolution or to borderless.
- **The saved resolution itself is a different one**: you changed it in Windows display settings or NVIDIA Control Panel. That is respected: Display Switch lets go of the competitive resolution, says so, and switches nothing more until the game is started again. Only NVIDIA's scaling setting is still put back.

As with HDR, it only undoes what it did: when the game closes, the display goes back only if it is still at the resolution Display Switch set. Pausing (**Ctrl+Alt+H**) puts everything back and keeps nothing until you resume.

**A stretched resolution inside the area.** A game can also run at a *lower* resolution of its own, stretched over the competitive one: 4:3 at 1776 × 1332 across a 2368 × 1332 area, say. On the game's page, click **Stretch** (under **Competitive area**, which it needs) and pick one; the list shows what fits inside the competitive resolution, and the picture of the screen then shows the stretched resolution at its own size inside the area. While the game runs, the desktop switches to that resolution and the GPU stretches it over the area, with the same black borders around it. The monitor still gets its native signal, and the stretching is done by the scaler in the GPU's display output, the one "Full screen" scaling uses, only aimed at a smaller rectangle. Nothing is added to the display path. Three things follow from how it works:

- **The game has to be in borderless mode** (*Fullscreen Windowed* in Counter-Strike 2, *Windowed Fullscreen* in Valorant). There it draws at the desktop's resolution and asks Windows for nothing. A game in *exclusive* fullscreen sets a display mode of its own, and Windows then goes back to the driver's scaling: with the No scaling that Display Switch sets while the game runs, a 1:1 box in the middle instead of the whole panel. Display Switch doesn't fight over the display: it leaves the game's own mode alone and says so, in a notification and on the **Stretch** layer of the flyout's **Live** tab (*The game set its own mode since*). Switch the game to borderless and start it again. A borderless game that covers the screen is normally sent straight to the display on Windows 11, as exclusive fullscreen is; anything drawn over it (a notification, an overlay) goes through Windows' compositor while it shows.
- **The resolution has to fit inside the competitive one.** The GPU's scaler only enlarges. A resolution that would have to shrink (1920 × 1440 into 2368 × 1332) is done by Windows' compositor instead, which costs latency, so it is neither offered nor applied. The largest 4:3 inside 2368 × 1332 is 1776 × 1332.
- **Everything is stretched while the game runs**, the desktop and the windows you Alt+Tab to included, because the desktop itself is at the game's resolution.

Unlike the plain competitive resolution, Windows doesn't drop this mode by itself if Display Switch is killed mid-game: Display Switch puts the screen back the next time it starts, and so does a reboot or sign-out, since the mode is never saved. NVIDIA's scaling is set to No scaling for these games too, so a stretch that is lost degrades to a centred box. This was developed and tested on an NVIDIA card; another graphics driver may refuse it, in which case the plain competitive resolution is used and a notification says so ([#1](https://github.com/sxnfer/game-display-switch/issues/1)).

The profiles live in `%AppData%\HdrSwitch\resolutions.txt`, one game per line (`cs2.exe  2368x1332`, optionally `@240` for a refresh rate and `stretch 1776x1332` for the game's own resolution); the pencil on the game's page opens it, and edits apply as soon as you save.

**When it can't be had exactly.** Display Switch looks at what the display offers and takes the nearest thing that keeps the area, in this order:

- **The refresh rate.** A size is switched at the profile's `@` rate, or without one at the desktop's current rate. Windows counts in whole Hz, and the same 239.76 Hz can show as 239 on one size and 240 on another, so one Hz above or below counts as the same rate (143 for 144, 239 for 240) and is used without a word. If the size doesn't exist there either and the profile names no rate, it is switched at the highest rate it has that isn't above the desktop's, and a notification says which (*2368 × 1332 doesn't exist at 240 Hz: playing at 144 Hz.*). A profile with `@240` is never switched at another rate.
- **The stretch is given up before the rate.** If the stretched resolution doesn't exist at the rate but the competitive one does, the game plays at the plain competitive resolution, 1:1 in the middle (*1776 × 1332 at 240 Hz doesn't exist yet: playing at 2368 × 1332 without the stretch.*). Create the missing size and the next start stretches again.
- **A stretch the driver doesn't carry out.** Right after setting it, Display Switch checks that the desktop really is stretched over the area. If it isn't, it looks again half a second later and sets it once more; if it still isn't, or Windows refused it outright, the plain competitive resolution is used and a notification says so.
- **Nothing fits:** the display stays as it is and a notification says what to create ([Creating the resolution](#creating-the-resolution)).

Each of these is said once per start of the game, not once and never again, and the latest one stays on the game's page. The area of a stretch is measured on the panel's own (native) resolution, also when the monitor happens to be getting another signal at that moment.

**If a game starts at the wrong size** (full screen at your normal resolution, a box that isn't the size you chose, a stretch that didn't happen), open the flyout's **Settings › Switch log**. Each decision is one line: look for the `apply` line at the time the game started, which names the mode wanted, the mode that was there and why it was or wasn't switched, its `plan` (as wanted, another rate, without the stretch, nothing), then `scaling-take` (NVIDIA's scaling, and whether it read back as set), `picture` (whether the result is centred or stretched), `stretch-verify` and `fallback` (a stretch that didn't show and what was used instead) and any `display-changed`, `mode-check` and `reapply` line while the game ran: they say when the resolution was lost under the game and what was done about it. [Using it](using-it.md#the-flyout) says what each line means.
